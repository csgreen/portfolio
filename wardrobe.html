Independent product experiment · 2026

Building an AI wardrobe that remembers what matters.

An evolving personal styling workflow that connects real inventory, visual references, color analysis, constraints, and corrective feedback—with data reliability and user trust as first-class product requirements.

01 · Problem

Advice is only as good as its context.

Generic styling recommendations can look convincing and still be unusable. The system needs to know what is actually owned—not simply what was purchased—what is rented or returned, which colors flatter the wearer, and which clothes fit the day.

The challenge is not generating outfit ideas. It's producing trustworthy recommendations across changing, partially structured information: receipts, photos, wear feedback, fit, weather, laundering, and personal preferences.

02 · Approach

Design around a changing source of truth.

I treated the experience as a connected workflow rather than a single prompt. These are the four product layers developed through iteration.

01 / INGEST

Capture

Receipts, purchase details, product imagery, and manual wardrobe corrections.

02 / STRUCTURE

Reconcile

Item IDs, categories, colors, ownership state, purchase provenance, and image links.

03 / PERSONALIZE

Recommend

Factor in actual pieces, color palette, silhouette, occasion, shoes, socks, and accessories.

04 / LEARN

Correct

Record misses—wrong garment, invalid ownership, poor fit—and refine the next recommendation.

03 · Product decisions

Trust beats a good-looking wrong answer.

Separate purchase history from current ownership. The system must not recommend returned or sold clothing, and rentals must not silently become permanent inventory.

Preserve the evidence behind a recommendation. The workbook retains 183 embedded thumbnails in a fixed photo catalog so sorting the inventory doesn't mix up photos and garments.

Represent uncertainty explicitly. Palette assessments distinguish close matches, poor matches, and cases needing daylight verification—not every color deserves a forced label.

Accept corrections as product signals. Feedback revealed the need to rotate suggestions, respect dirty laundry, avoid generic images masquerading as exact garments, and specify practical outfit details.

04 · Findings

The interesting work is in the failure modes.

Data quality is a product feature.

Returns, duplicate purchases, missing images, and loosely identified items can produce confident but incorrect advice. Explicit statuses and provenance reduce those errors.

Personalization requires more than memory.

Preferences and garment constraints change. Recommendations need a maintained source of truth rather than relying on a model's recollection alone.

Visual grounding matters.

Lookalike product photography is not interchangeable with the user's actual wardrobe. Exact images—and honest caveats when unavailable—are part of the trust contract.

Qualitative feedback suggests the next experiments.

Tracking repeat suggestions, correction frequency, and real-world outfit acceptance would help evaluate recommendation quality beyond aesthetic appeal.

05 · Scope & next iteration

A working personal workflow, not a shipped SaaS product.

The artifacts include a structured Excel wardrobe inventory, embedded item imagery, annotated color guidance, and iterative ChatGPT-assisted recommendation workflows. This is a self-directed personal experiment; no external user adoption, revenue, or automated end-to-end integration is claimed.

Next: add sanitized real screenshots, document before/after recommendation examples, define a small evaluation rubric, and test whether retrieval from the inventory consistently improves precision over memory-only prompts.

The product lesson: impressive generation is easy to demonstrate; reliable relevance requires deliberate systems and feedback.
