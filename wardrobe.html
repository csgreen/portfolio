Independent AI product exploration · 2026

From conversation to system.

I wanted to spend less time sorting through information and more time making good decisions. So I tested what AI could do.

Applied AI · Product thinking · Human judgment

The problem

Too much information. Too much effort.

I had good information, but it was scattered. I kept having to remember rules and piece things together.

What I built

A working experiment, not a polished app.

I used ChatGPT and a Google Sheets inventory, then tested the results in everyday decisions.

What mattered

Getting it right mattered most.

A useful answer needs accurate information, a clear explanation, and room for a person to say, “That’s not right.”

The experiment

Start with something real.

I used personal presentation as my test case. It was small enough to try things quickly, but complex enough to be interesting: changing plans, real constraints, visual choices, and lots of scattered information.

What I built

Build, use, learn.

I started with the tools I had. Each time something went wrong, I looked for a practical fix.

01

Understand the decision

Figure out what matters, what changes, and what can’t be ignored.

02

Bring the pieces together

Put records, images, and helpful rules within reach.

03

Pay attention to mistakes

Notice where the answer fails, ask why, and change the workflow.

What broke — and what changed

The mistakes shaped the product.

Each wrong answer told me what the system was missing.

Problem

The assistant sometimes used outdated information.

→

Change

Keep a Google Sheets inventory I can check and update.

Problem

Generated visuals sometimes showed the wrong items.

→

Change

Use photos of the real items wherever possible.

Problem

A good suggestion one day could be wrong the next.

→

Change

Check current plans, weather, and temporary constraints.

Problem

It was tempting to automate more before the recommendations were dependable.

→

Change

Keep a person in charge until the basics are reliable.

What stayed with me

An answer is only useful if you can trust it.

The best improvements weren’t flashy. They helped me notice errors, make corrections, and choose with more confidence.

What the experiment demonstrates

AI fluency, built through practice.

I used AI as a product-building partner—not just a way to generate answers. The work was in designing the inputs, testing the output, spotting failure modes, and deciding where a human should stay in control.

01

Grounding & data reliability

Move important records into an editable source of truth, rather than relying on conversational recall alone.

02

Context & state

Separate persistent preferences from things that change, like availability, timing, or new constraints.

03

Human-in-the-loop design

Make suggestions explainable and easy to correct. Keep the final decision with the person using the system.

04

Evaluation through use

Use real errors to improve the workflow. Next, add repeatable measures of accuracy, relevance, and decision time.

Current scope: a working ChatGPT + Google Sheets workflow with manual review. Automated synchronization, formal evaluation, and autonomous actions remain future opportunities.

Where this pattern can help

Useful across the product lifecycle.

The same ingredients—scattered evidence, expert rules, changing context, and human decisions—show up throughout product work and the software development lifecycle.

⌕

≡

✎

</>

Bring specs, technical context, and standards into decisions.

↗

Check readiness against dependencies and agreed criteria.

↻

Iterate

Use feedback and performance signals to refine the next choice.

These are potential applications of the decision-support pattern, not claims that this prototype has been deployed across those stages.

The result today

A useful working workflow

I can bring information together, compare options, and see why a recommendation fits. I still review the result, and I haven’t yet measured time saved or recommendation quality.

The product opportunity

Less busywork. Better judgment.

Good decision support makes expertise available when people need it. It should make teams more effective without hiding uncertainty or taking away their agency.
