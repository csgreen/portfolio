Flipper Finder.

Turning a mountain of lost-and-found photos into something parents can actually search—without making kids' privacy an afterthought.

01 / The problem

The photos were there. Finding anything was the hard part.

At my kids' elementary school, volunteers photographed lost-and-found piles for a shared album. Parents still had to visit in person—or scroll through photos of heaps of hoodies and hope they spotted something familiar.

The album also included name labels. I wanted to make existing photos searchable with a description like “navy fleece, size 8,” while protecting kids' information more carefully.

I built this as a parent volunteer, not as an official school or PTSA project. That made the constraints clear: keep it inexpensive, easy to maintain, and safe by default.

Many photos. No search.

03 / The judgment calls

Ship what helps a parent recognize their item.

I didn't try to perfect every edge case. I focused on accuracy, privacy, and the moments that make the product useful. Everything else needed a reason to earn its way into the MVP.

Protect first

Nothing goes public automatically.

Every upload goes through my review. That let me see how AI performed on messy photos before families saw the result.

Decision: keep a human approval gate.

Optimize for recognition

Crops should show the whole item.

I adjusted cropping three times. I blurred faces and name labels, not every hand, foot, or shoe tip—while continuing to flag people for review.

Decision: make the image useful, not just technically processed.

Measure before building

Park the extra controls.

Rather than add Split and Zoom-out buttons immediately, I added counters: build Split if more than 5% of items are merged; Zoom-out if more than 10% are cropped too tightly.

Decision: let actual failure rates set the roadmap.

Follow the parent's mental model

Group by when the photo was taken.

Parents remember when something went missing, not when a volunteer uploaded a photo.

Decision: sort by photo date, not upload date.

Deliberate cuts: Video, alerts, Google Photos import, and automatic deletion moved to V1; a family password moved to V2. iPhone HEIC uploads show a clear JPG-conversion message rather than requiring an extra server service. I made these choices against a two-hour launch window and estimates that included testing.

04 / Privacy by design

Care for people shows up in the requirements.

I wrote privacy rules into the brief before coding. In a tool for families, protecting children wasn't a feature to add later.

Private originals, reviewed public copies

Original photos stay private. Parents see resized, blurred images and item crops only after approval.

Useful labels, hidden names

Label text can support matching but lives in restricted storage. Searches reveal only whether a name label matches, not its text.

No parent tracking

No parent accounts, analytics, ads, or trackers. Search queries aren't stored or logged; fonts are bundled.

Least access and clear controls

Volunteer access uses a changeable secret link. Admin review is limited to my Google account; secrets are held in Firebase's secret manager. Every item has a report link.

A real correction changed the product.

While making screenshots for the guides, I found an approved image showing children wearing items at a front door. Their faces were blurred, but the image didn't belong in the public collection. I removed it that hour and revised the guides to describe only what the system actually guarantees.

Search engines are asked not to index the app, and outbound links avoid passing its address. These measures reduce exposure; they do not replace authentication. A family password is planned for V2.

05 / Working with AI

Claude wrote the code. I owned the product decisions.

I was new to Firebase and the command line. I used Claude Code to build faster without giving up responsibility for the user experience, security, or what shipped.

Start with a clear brief

A one-page CLAUDE.md defined the product, privacy and matching rules, stack, and eight-step build order. Sessions started from that source of truth.

Set approval gates

Claude explained work in plain language and asked before cloud changes, costs, or deployment. I handled sign-ins, secrets, and security decisions.

Test what people actually see

I compared 20 searches against the prototype and checked every deployment live. Reviewing real images revealed crop, person-detection, and cache issues tests missed.

Choose, don't just prompt

I requested estimates including testing and decided whether to build, instrument and defer, or cut each idea.

Preserve context between sessions

A roadmap, handoff notes, and project memory kept decisions from getting lost or repeatedly reopened.

Know where AI falls short

AI sped up implementation and cataloging. Human judgment remained essential for privacy, visual quality, and launch readiness.

06 / Results + what's next

A working MVP, ready for volunteer testing.

The product went live in four days, November 8–11, 2026. At the time of this case study, it was moving to volunteer testing before a broader parent-group launch—not yet a measured adoption success.

What helped

Clarity created speed.

A functioning prototype, a one-page brief, and the habit of parking work with a tracking signal kept the MVP small enough to ship.

What surprised me

Cloud setup cost more time than code.

Security defaults, expired sign-ins, and a rejected API key created 15–45-minute delays. Those constraints now belong in the estimates—not outside them.

Next, in order: protection against other sites calling the app, Google Photos import, video, alerts, and clearing items after donation runs. A family password is planned for V2.
