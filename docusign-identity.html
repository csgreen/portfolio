Making the case to retire social login.

A vendor renewal surfaced an uncomfortable question: was a lightly used sign-in option worth keeping if removing it might lock customers out? I built the evidence for deprecation and helped leadership agree to ship it.

Why we looked

A renewal became a product decision.

My manager asked me to investigate an upcoming contract renewal for our social-login provider. The service appeared to have several issues and little usage, but removing a sign-in method could have serious consequences. Leadership worried that customers would lose access or face a more difficult login experience.

The easy answer would have been to renew and avoid the risk. I wanted to understand how many customers truly depended on the feature before we committed to another contract.

The evidence

Measure dependence, not just usage.

I investigated how customers actually accessed their accounts, along with the signals that might indicate business or customer risk.

Alternative paths to sign inVery few accounts used social login exclusively; most social-login users also had an email address associated with the account.
Support signalsI found essentially no customer-service complaints associated with social login.
Enterprise exposureThe largest customer account with a social-login user had very low platform spend, limiting the apparent commercial exposure.
Leadership's concernThe possibility of lockouts or added sign-in friction remained important even when the affected population was small.

An email address associated with an account is a useful risk indicator, but it does not by itself prove that every user has a working alternative sign-in method. That distinction mattered when assessing migration risk.

My decision and influence

Turn the analysis into a recommendation.

I developed and advocated the case for deprecation. Rather than arguing from the low overall usage alone, I reframed the discussion around how many customers actually depended on this sign-in path, the potential consequences of removing it, and the evidence we had about support and business exposure.

The disagreement

Leadership initially favored protecting access by keeping the option available. They were concerned a removal would make sign-in harder and create support issues.

What I drove

I gathered account, customer-support, and commercial evidence and used it to persuade stakeholders that retiring the feature was an acceptable product decision.

The product judgment: don't pay indefinitely to preserve a feature simply because removing it feels risky. Identify who depends on it, evaluate the real risk, and make the decision explicit.

What happened

We removed it. Support volume did not rise.

The social-login removal shipped, and customer-support volume did not increase afterward. That was the outcome leadership had been most worried about.

It reinforced a leadership lesson I've used since: when a decision feels risky, find the smallest set of evidence that directly tests the concern. A good recommendation earns agreement by addressing the strongest objection.

Not claimed here: a quantified cost saving, zero individual lockouts, or a measured improvement in sign-in success. The available evidence supports the shipped removal and no increase in overall support volume.

Broader identity experience

The context behind this decision.

This was one decision within my larger DocuSign identity work. I also owned authentication roadmap and migration work involving OAuth and SAML, collaborated across more than 20 engineering teams, and helped modernize login experiences through two-step verification and legacy-authentication changes.

The wider program's scale and outcomes should not be attributed to the social-login removal itself.
